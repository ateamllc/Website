The Tools Every DIYer Actually Needs (and What We’d Buy With $5,000)

Lessons from years of buying, losing, and re-buying tools on real jobs. Start here before you fill a garage.

By McKay Whiting and Elijah Updated: November 19, 2026 Estimated read: 7 min

The Tools Every DIYer Actually Needs (and What We’d Buy With $5,000)

Quick answer: If you are starting a basic tool kit, buy an impact driver, a small circular saw, an oscillating multi-tool, a speed square, two levels, a tape measure, and a good supply of screws and wall anchors. Plan your batteries early, because they cost more than the tools. Then organize and store everything so you can find it. Rent the one-off tools.

We learned most of this the expensive way. These are the tools we would buy again, the ones we wish we had bought sooner, and the mistakes worth skipping.

The short list

  • Impact driver. In today’s world you hardly need a separate drill if you have an impact driver. Buy impact-rated bits that also drill, so one tool does both jobs.
  • Circular saw. A smaller one is plenty, and used ones are cheap on local marketplaces. A circular saw can do nearly everything a chop saw can, in a fraction of the space.
  • Oscillating multi-tool. The one we wish we had owned much earlier. It handles flush cuts, trim, and awkward spaces that nothing else reaches.
  • Reciprocating saw and a jigsaw for demolition and curved cuts.
  • Tape measure and a speed square. Aluminum speed squares are cheap and underrated.
  • Two levels: a small magnetic one and a 4-foot one.
  • Drywall anchors and a large assortment of screws. Good anchors go a long way.
  • Cutting tools: a good knife and a good pair of scissors.
  • Yard tools: a shovel is incredibly versatile, and a wheelbarrow is underrated.
  • Markers and pencils. Buy more than you think.

Batteries are the real cost

Once you have a few cordless tools, the batteries become the constraint. They are more expensive than the tools themselves, and they add up quickly, especially if more than one person is working. Plan ahead: nothing is worse than a crew of four with every battery dead at once and nothing to do while they charge. One of the best ways to get batteries is a bundle deal from a home-improvement store where the tool comes with batteries. We bought a larger circular saw in a bundle that included two batteries, and it was a great deal.

Organization beats owning more

For me, the biggest lesson was this: you have to own your tools, and then you have to have them available. We started with about twenty big totes. They were heavy, they did not stack well, they would not roll, and some of them broke under the weight. Switching to a modular rolling storage system (like Milwaukee Packout, or the more budget-friendly store-brand versions of it) was a game changer. Small totes inside it let you grab a set, like a screwdriver set or a fastener set, and go.

  • A tool bag at your waist with a tape measure, marker, square, and screws saves endless trips back to the truck.
  • Keep one of each bit with you, and a few extras of the common ones. Nothing wastes time like hunting for a bit.
  • Keep a fastener stash in the vehicle. You will be surprised how often you need a random screw.
  • Pack a painting tote with everything for a touch-up, so you are not sifting through supplies.

The underrated one: a portable vacuum

A cordless vacuum, such as one from Milwaukee, is not a glamorous tool, but homeowners love seeing a clean job site when you leave. Being able to clean up anywhere is a game changer.

What we would buy with $5,000

If we had to rebuild a tool inventory from scratch with $5,000, we would buy almost everything above, in this order of thinking:

  1. The core cordless tools (impact driver, circular saw, oscillating tool), on bundles that include batteries.
  2. Measuring, marking, and leveling tools, which are cheap and used on every job.
  3. Fasteners and anchors, in volume.
  4. A storage system, decided before buying more tools. Ask how you will store and move them day to day, and whether you need everything you already own.
  5. A lean, multi-purpose inventory. Prefer tools that fill several roles: impact bits that also drill, and a circular saw instead of a chop saw.

What we would rent instead

We bought one-off tools we only used once. A router is a good example: we could easily have used it again, but in practice we used it only one time. If a job needs a tool you will use once, rent it. The less you own, the less you have to store, maintain, and keep track of.

Two lessons about a paint sprayer

A paint sprayer is on both our best and worst lists. It made a handful of jobs easier and quicker, including a few fence refinishes and some interior work. Poor maintenance cost us, and we had to buy another one partway through a job. If you buy a sprayer, clean it properly after every use.

Frequently asked questions

What are the first tools a beginner DIYer should buy?

An impact driver, a small circular saw, a tape measure, a speed square, two levels, a good assortment of screws and drywall anchors, and a knife. Add an oscillating multi-tool next.

Do I need both a drill and an impact driver?

Not usually. An impact driver with impact-rated bits that also drill covers most home projects.

Why are batteries such a big part of the cost?

Cordless tools need several batteries to keep working, and the batteries often cost more than the tool. Bundles that include batteries are one of the best ways to buy.

Should I buy or rent specialty tools?

Rent any tool you will use once. Buy the ones you will use on most jobs.
