What a Handyman Can (and Can’t) Do

Most home repair jobs are a handyman’s kind of job. A few are not. Here is how to tell the difference before you call.

By McKay Whiting and Elijah Updated: November 19, 2026 Estimated read: 5 min

What a Handyman Can (and Can’t) Do

Quick answer: A handyman is the right call for small and mid-sized repair and improvement jobs: drywall patches, painting, trim, doors, mounting, assembly, and fences. Electrical work, plumbing, and structural changes belong with the licensed trades. When you are unsure, ask the contractor what they will and will not do, and check Utah’s official licensing site.

What a handyman is good at

Most of the calls we get are for the same kind of work: the list of small things that has been sitting on the fridge for months, or a repair that has to be done right before a home is sold or rented. That is exactly what a handyman is for. Typical jobs include:

  • Drywall repair: patches, texture matching, and getting a wall ready to paint
  • Interior and exterior painting, including the prep that decides how the paint looks
  • Trim and baseboard, door repair, and small carpentry
  • TV mounting, furniture assembly, and the rest of a honey-do list
  • Fences and gates

These jobs have something in common: the scope is clear, the work is visible, and a mistake can be fixed. That is why they suit one capable crew with the right tools.

What we hand to a licensed trade

One request comes up more than any other. Almost everyone assumes a handyman can do electrical and plumbing work, and we cannot. If it involves wiring, a panel, a water line, or a drain, call a licensed electrician or plumber. The same goes for structural work, such as changing a load-bearing wall or a beam. We turn those jobs down, and we say so on the first call so nobody wastes a week.

Framing is a good example of where the line sits. We do non-structural framing, such as non-load-bearing interior walls and window and door framing. We do not do structural or load-bearing framing.

It is tempting to treat a light fixture or a faucet as a small swap. Behind the fixture there is wiring or a pressurized line, and that is where the trades earn their license. If a job starts as a handyman job and turns up a wiring or plumbing problem, the right move is to stop, tell the homeowner, and bring in the licensed trade.

The gray areas

Some jobs sit between the two. A bathroom refresh, for example, can be mostly paint, trim, and fixtures, with one part that needs a plumber. A good handyman will split the job: do the part that fits, and tell you plainly which part needs someone else. Ask for that split in writing so the scope is clear on both sides.

How to check before you hire

  • Ask what the contractor will not do. A clear answer is a good sign.
  • Ask for the license number and look it up with the state. Utah’s official source is the Utah Division of Professional Licensing (DOPL). It also explains what requires a license.
  • Get the scope, the price, and the warranty in writing. A Team’s labor warranty and terms are in our payment and terms.

Frequently asked questions

Can a handyman do electrical work?

No. Wiring, panels, and anything that touches your electrical system belongs with a licensed electrician. We refer those jobs out.

Can a handyman do plumbing?

Not the plumbing itself. Water lines, drains, and fixtures that are tied into them go to a licensed plumber. We can handle the surrounding repair, such as patching and painting after the plumber is done.

What should a handyman refuse to do?

Work that needs a specialty license, changes the structure of the home, or that they do not have the tools and training to do safely. A good handyman says so up front.

How do I check a contractor’s license in Utah?

Use the Utah Division of Professional Licensing (DOPL) website, the state’s official source for licensing information.
